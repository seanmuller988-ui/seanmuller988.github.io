Quantitative methods

Math and statistics in the research process

The mathematical and statistical tools I use to decide whether a result is real: what problem each one answers, why the naive version can fail, and where it sits in the research.

How I think: the research loop

The loop below is how a question becomes a claim, and most of the work sits in steps four to seven.

  1. QuestionA specific question about market behavior, or about the evidence itself.
  2. HypothesisWhat I expect to see and the economic reason for it.
  3. Design the testBaseline, metric, placebo and failure criteria, written before the result.
  4. Try to break itPlacebos, dependence-aware inference, multiple-testing correction, modeled costs.
  5. DiagnoseWhen it fails, find out why: the data, the method, or the idea.
  6. ReviseChange the method or drop the claim, and record what changed.
  7. RetestRerun against the same controls, on the same build and data, and count the rerun as another trial.
  8. Forward and live evidenceThe last layer: evidence that selection could not have touched.

A test is designed before its result is read: the baseline it has to beat, the placebo that has to fail, and the outcome that would count against the idea. A result then has to survive dependent observations, the number of variants tried, and costs. One written prediction, that a ratio “will not be about” a given value, turned out to be satisfied by an outcome and by its opposite, so predictions are now written as the interval they forbid.

Clearing a bar is not enough by itself. In August 2026 a result from an earlier line of work passed, on unseen dates, the bar set for it in advance. I shelved it anyway: I could not say why it should work, a few large wins carried the average, most of the effect sat in two months, and it was the only survivor of a large family of trials.

The last step is the one no backtest can supply. Forward and live evidence is the only kind that selection inside the research window cannot have touched. It is ongoing work, and no forward or live result is shown on this site.

Research standards, and the events behind them

Each rule below is tied to the event that produced it, in the order the rules were adopted between May and September 2026. Select a rule to read the event behind it.

  1. May 2026

    Count events, not rows

    A result is counted in independent events, not rows, and three events are too few to estimate anything.

    A backtest passed its robustness test on a few dozen trades. A closer look at the rows found three distinct events, each counted many times.

    A later result logged the same day looked much stronger in aggregate and was recorded as illusory by this standard, because it rested on a handful of events.

  2. June 2026

    Ledger, deflation, one holdout read

    I decided that every searched cell is logged, failures included, that the bar applies only after deflating for the full count, and that the final holdout is read once.

    A planned search covered hundreds of configurations against one fixed quality bar. Across that many cells, chance clears a fixed bar somewhere.

    Two weeks later a search of 156 settings was reported with its full count, and none cleared on held-out data. The rule lapsed in the next line of work; the counting-trials case study is the correction.

  3. June 2026

    Say which stages are real

    No backtest result is called trustworthy unless its controls pass and it comes with a list of which stages are real, stand-in or absent, and the report leads with what has not been checked.

    My questions about how the backtest of an earlier line of work was built led to a stage-by-stage audit. It was a parallel re-implementation: one of six stages ran the production code, four ran simplified stand-ins and one was missing.

    From July it was a written standing rule for quoting any figure.

  4. June 2026

    Planted effect and noise first

    A candidate statistic is admissible only if it finds a planted effect and reads as chance on planted noise, and that is settled before its score on real data is read.

    I chose a candidate statistic because it scored best on real data. Four days later a negative control on one small synthetic sample read it above chance on pure noise.

    The statistic I had chosen was set aside that day. One small sample was enough to apply the rule; it does not measure the size of a leak, and one reading cannot exclude chance. Planted-noise controls appear in more than thirty later research reports.

  5. June 2026

    Attack the best result first

    The strongest result is attacked first, and every selection step upstream of a model is held to the same point-in-time rule as the model.

    Within a day of the best result so far, I asked for a search for errors in it by as many routes as possible. An upstream selection step had been computed on the full history, test periods included; recomputed on training data only, one of the two headline settings held and the other fell back to its baseline.

  6. July 2026

    Test on data predating the rule

    A new rule has to hold on a different, longer period, on data generated before the rule existed; agreement between halves of the sample it was found on is not enough.

    Two candidate rule changes held in both halves of the history they were found on. I asked for a test on a different, longer period. It used records generated before either rule existed. One replicated and the other reversed sign.

    I later asked the same of an entry filter: see the entry-filter case study.

  7. August 2026

    Review without the reasoning

    A finding on the main strategy is not accepted until a second review pass, given only the data and the claim, has tried to refute it.

    A strong result in another line of work had passed an overfitting battery. A second review pass that I asked for saw only the data and the claim, not the reasoning behind it. It found that every control drew from one hindsight-filtered sample, and the headline did not survive.

    Two days later such a pass reproduced a study’s arithmetic exactly and overturned its inference.

  8. August 2026

    Reproduce a known result first

    A study tool has to reproduce a known result on the original stocks before any number from new territory is read.

    A study reported that the strategy did not carry over to a much wider set of stocks. I did not believe an empty result across that many names. The audit found a screen applied in the wrong unit, so only about a fifth of the new stocks had been tested, and stock splits missing from the data.

    The control failed once more after the tool was rebuilt, exposing a third defect.

  9. September 2026

    Score net of market exposure

    Holding-period, exit and sizing comparisons are scored on return net of fitted market exposure, with the raw figure beside it, against what the freed capital could earn.

    On an earlier configuration, a test of shorter holding periods on a long-only book showed the shorter variants losing, and the question was closed. I objected: in a rising market such a book collects drift for as long as it holds, so a test scored on raw return can only say hold longer.

    Re-read that way on an earlier configuration, about half of the apparent cost of shorter holds was market drift, and part of the closed verdict became inconclusive.

Evaluation design

Before any statistic is computed, these methods settle what a backtest can be evidence for: models fitted on earlier data and scored only on what follows, each input checked for whether it was knowable on the date it was used, and splits that keep overlapping outcomes on one side. A walk-forward test closes one leak only; the choice among configurations, the stock list, the data and the fill assumptions can each carry hindsight of their own.

Why it matters here. My first reported backtest was walk-forward for its models but read its stock list from a present-day snapshot; rebuilt as of each date, with modeled fills, the two-year result went from a superseded 4.76× to 1.87×. In the ranking feasibility study, a repeatability score of 0.48 on a random split fell to 0.02 when the same data were split by blocks of time.

Walk-forward evaluation, and what it does not protect

A rule fitted and scored on the same data looks better than it will on new data.

Why a naive method can fail
An in-sample backtest scores fitted parameters on outcomes they have seen. Walk-forward testing closes that leak only: the choice among configurations, the stock list, the data and the fill assumptions can still carry hindsight.
The method
Walk-forward evaluation: models are fitted on earlier data, scored only on the period that follows, and rolled forward.
How it fits the research
Every backtest on this site uses it. Fitted parameters are out of sample in a walk-forward test; the choice among configurations is not. My first reported backtest took its stock list from a present-day snapshot, so each input now gets its own as-of-date question.

Applied in: Restating a backtest whose stock universe was built with hindsight.

Point-in-time data and look-ahead tests

A backtest can use information that was unavailable at decision time without raising an error.

Why a naive method can fail
Results that use future data look better, so nothing prompts a check. A check that shares code with what it checks can pass for the same wrong reason.
The method
Point-in-time reconstruction asks of each input whether it was knowable on the date it was used. Automated tests assert that fitting data ends before each test period, and that a statistic’s value on a given date is the same whether it is computed from history cut off at that date or from the full history.
How it fits the research
My first reported backtest failed that question on its stock universe and was restated lower. An earlier look-ahead audit passed because it derived its dates the same way as the calendar it checked.

Applied in: An entry filter designed around a set of losing trades did no better than random filters on earlier data.

Time-blocked splits and overlapping outcomes

Trades open at the same time share part of their outcome, so they are not independent observations.

Why a naive method can fail
A random split puts near-copies of one outcome on both sides and inflates the score. The raw count of observations also overstates the information in the sample.
The method
Splits by blocks of time, and an effective sample size in place of the raw count: observations are discounted for overlap, and variances are inflated by a design effect measured with a bootstrap over whole days.
How it fits the research
In the feasibility study on ranking, a random split-half test reported a repeatability of 0.48. Split by alternating blocks of time, the same statistic was 0.02. The change was logged as an amendment to the study’s plan.

Applied in: Measuring whether ranking was possible before building another ranker.

Dependence-aware inference

Daily returns and overlapping trades are not independent draws, so intervals that assume independence come out too narrow and a trade count overstates the information in the sample. These methods keep the dependence in the standard errors: block and stationary bootstraps over whole sessions, cluster-robust errors quoted with their cluster counts, and paired session-by-session differences when two backtests are compared.

Why it matters here. A rule change had been logged as the one to bring forward on the strength of two backtests; a panel of paired differences could not separate it from its reference, and the entry was corrected as premature. Where a correction was small, that is reported too: session-level and week-level intervals on the cost and execution statistics changed the sign of no conclusion.

Block bootstrap for dependent return series

Putting an interval on a Sharpe ratio or an annual return, and a range on a drawdown, when daily returns are dependent.

Why a naive method can fail
Resampling single observations assumes independence. Overlapping holding periods and persistent volatility make neighboring observations dependent, so iid intervals can be too narrow.
The method
Block-bootstrap inference is used where temporal dependence makes iid resampling inappropriate: moving blocks of sessions, or the stationary bootstrap of Politis and Romano, with an automatic block length and longer blocks reported beside it.
How it fits the research
It replaced the iid intervals used earlier and drives the family-wise tests below. A rule written into the sweep-wide test’s plan classed a conclusion that changes with block length as unstable, not significant.

Applied in: Counting the trials behind a deflated Sharpe ratio.

Cluster-robust standard errors and serial-dependence diagnostics

Trades entered in the same session or week share market shocks and carry less independent information than their count suggests.

Why a naive method can fail
Treating each trade as independent understates the standard error. A blind correction hides whether dependence matters. The cluster-robust estimator has its own limit: with a handful of clusters it is biased downward and the normal reference distribution is wrong.
The method
Diagnostics first: autocorrelation of daily returns and of absolute returns, the share of overlapping trades, and the intraclass correlation of outcomes within a session. Cluster bootstraps that resample whole sessions or whole weeks, and cluster-robust standard errors, one-way and two-way. Every clustered t-statistic is quoted with its cluster count. Rows with few clusters are labeled unreliable, not corrected.
How it fits the research
The diagnostics set the resampling unit and say whether a correction matters. Cost and execution statistics were restated with session-level and week-level intervals beside the iid one. The correction was small and no conclusion changed sign.

Applied in: Testing my own hypothesis about crowded chart triggers until no trading claim was left.

Comparing two backtests: paired differences and compounding noise

The gain from a rule change is a difference between two backtests, and a compounded ending multiple is path-dependent.

Why a naive method can fail
Comparing ending values mixes the effect of the change with path noise: one early trade shifts everything after it. It also ignores that both arms trade mostly the same sessions.
The method
The paired difference between candidate and reference, session by session, with a dependence-aware interval, so noise common to both arms cancels. For the multiple’s noise, the same rules rerun from neighboring start dates, and the spread between runs that differ only by an arbitrary change or a placebo.
How it fits the research
A candidate rule change was logged as the one to bring forward after two backtests. A panel of paired differences could not separate it from its reference, and the entry was corrected as premature. A gap is read against what small, arbitrary changes do to a two-year compounding multiple. Cost variants are compared on per-order cost and matched trades.

Multiple testing and selection bias

When many variants are tried on one sample, the best of them is biased upward by the selection, and any correction needs an honest count of what was tried. These methods count the trials and estimate how many were effectively independent, test the best candidate jointly with all the others against a benchmark fixed in advance, and place a selected Sharpe ratio on a probability scale against the best that luck alone would produce.

Why it matters here. My first reported backtest deflated its Sharpe ratio for an assumed 100 trials; a recount of the record found about 540 runs in 62 hypothesis families, and the claim was restated as a range. The October 2026 sweep was then judged once, as one family of 69 candidates beside 44 placebo twins: one survived on the whole sample, an effect already known, and it did not hold in the later half of the sample taken alone.

Trial accounting and the effective number of independent trials

A multiplicity correction needs a count of trials. Backtests of related variants are highly correlated, so neither the raw count nor one is right.

Why a naive method can fail
An assumed round number can understate the search. The raw count treats near-identical runs as independent, and a correlation-based count can understate the decisions taken.
The method
An inventory of every recorded run, grouped into hypothesis families, and an effective number of independent trials estimated several ways from the correlations between runs’ daily returns, reported as a range.
How it fits the research
My first reported backtest deflated its Sharpe ratio for an assumed 100 trials. A recount found about 540 simulation runs in 62 hypothesis families, and the claim was restated as a range.

Family-wise tests for the best of many candidates

Deciding whether the best of many candidates on one sample beats a benchmark or is only the luckiest.

Why a naive method can fail
A per-candidate t-test ignores that the maximum of many noisy statistics is biased upward. Bonferroni, the simple bound, loses power when candidates are correlated.
The method
White’s Reality Check and Hansen’s test of superior predictive ability bootstrap all candidates jointly, in blocks, against a benchmark fixed in advance. The Romano–Wolf step-down gives each an adjusted p-value.
How it fits the research
The October 2026 sweep was judged once, as one family: 69 candidates beside 44 placebo twins. One survived on the whole sample: an effect already known. It did not hold at the two longest block lengths, or in the later half of the sample taken alone.

Applied in: Counting the trials behind a deflated Sharpe ratio.

Probabilistic and deflated Sharpe ratio

A Sharpe ratio selected from many backtests on a short, fat-tailed sample is overstated.

Why a naive method can fail
The raw Sharpe ratio ignores sample length, skewness and kurtosis, and that the reported backtest was chosen from many trials.
The method
The probabilistic Sharpe ratio places an observed Sharpe ratio on a probability scale against a reference value, allowing for sample length, skewness and kurtosis. Bailey and López de Prado’s deflated version sets that reference to the best Sharpe expected by luck among the trials run, given how widely their Sharpe ratios vary. It is not a reduced Sharpe ratio, and it is not the probability that the strategy has skill. It is one minus the one-sided p-value for the hypothesis that the true Sharpe is no higher than the reference.
How it fits the research
For the selected configuration it is 0.85 against the 240 full-window backtests on file, below the conventional 0.95 bar; that count may understate the search.

Placebo controls, power and feasibility

A passing test means little unless it could have failed, and a null means little unless the test could have found an effect of the size that matters. These methods supply both sides: placebo and negative controls that keep the mechanics of a pipeline and remove its information, planted effects of known size to measure what the sample can detect, and a noise ceiling that says what a perfect model could reach before another model is built.

Why it matters here. In the October 2026 sweep the 44 placebo twins gave no rejections, as a null family should, while planted effects of two realistic sizes were found only about a fifth and about a third of the time, so most nulls in that sweep mean an effect of that size could not have been seen. Measuring the noise ceiling then closed a line of work as below resolution: the true spread between signals was more than ten times smaller than the noise in one outcome.

Placebo and negative controls, including placebo twins

A pipeline can report an effect that comes from its own mechanics, not from information.

Why a naive method can fail
A baseline alone cannot tell information from machinery. A placebo that breaks too much, such as pure noise, passes pipelines a subtler one would fail.
The method
Controls that keep the mechanics and remove the information: shuffled labels, scrambled inputs, a random draw in place of a rule’s information. Placebo twins of the candidates enter the same family-wise test and must fail.
How it fits the research
For the October 2026 sweep I required a named placebo in every pre-registration, and the 44 twins gave no rejections, as a null family should. One candidate rule change earned no credit: its placebos, one random seed each, mostly matched or beat it.

Applied in: Selecting on a noisy proxy: a result that looked real and was measurement error.

Planted-effect power analysis and the minimum detectable effect

A null means little unless the test could have found an effect of the size that matters.

Why a naive method can fail
Reporting only false-positive control hides the miss rate. A strict multiplicity rule can make every small real effect undetectable and look rigorous.
The method
Effects of known size are planted into simulated draws that keep the candidates’ measured correlations and standard errors. Each decision rule is applied and detections are counted. The smallest effect the sample could detect is stated.
How it fits the research
In the October 2026 sweep, planted effects of two realistic sizes were found about a fifth and about a third of the time at the studies’ own thresholds. The sample, not the threshold, limited discovery, so most nulls in that sweep mean an effect of that size could not have been seen, not that it is absent.

Applied in: Counting the trials behind a deflated Sharpe ratio.

The noise ceiling: what a perfect model could achieve

Knowing whether a prediction task is achievable with the data available, before building models.

Why a naive method can fail
Reading each null as a model failure cannot separate a weak model from an effect the sample cannot resolve.
The method
Split-half reliability on time-blocked halves estimates how much of the variation in outcomes is repeatable. The ratio of that repeatable spread to single-outcome noise sets the rank correlation a ranker that knew each group’s true expected value would reach. It is a ceiling for the groupings and the outcome examined, not for every possible input.
How it fits the research
After repeated failures to rank individual signals, the ceiling was measured before building another model. The true spread between signals was more than ten times smaller than the noise in one outcome. The pre-registered feasibility rule was not met, and the line was closed as below resolution.

Applied in: Measuring whether ranking was possible before building another ranker.

Learned models judged against placebo twins

A flexible learner may find structure that hand-written rules miss, or fit pure noise. Its scores have to order outcomes and add to a simple baseline.

Why a naive method can fail
Against only a weak baseline, a learned model gets credit for whatever the fitting procedure hands out. One good held-out half can be luck, and linear correlation is dominated by a few extreme outcomes.
The method
Gradient-boosted trees, a pairwise ranker and regularized linear models, tested as candidate rankers with nested chronological tuning, and scored by Spearman rank correlation with realized outcomes and by partial rank correlation controlling for a simple baseline. Each has placebo twins: the same recipe fitted on scrambled inputs, shuffled labels or pure noise.
How it fits the research
These were probes in the October 2026 sweep. Every rank correlation with outcomes was in the hundredths. No learned ranker beat the standing baseline on held-out data, and none was adopted. Measuring the noise ceiling then closed the line as below resolution.

Applied in: Measuring whether ranking was possible before building another ranker.

Return, risk and attribution statistics

A backtest reduces to a few comparable numbers only if each carries its convention: how the Sharpe ratio is annualized, which curve the drawdown is measured on, and how skewness and fat tails enter the sampling error. These methods also ask where the profit came from: how concentrated it is in a few trades, and how much of it is exposure to the market and to known factors rather than anything specific to the strategy.

Why it matters here. The concentration measures produced a limit stated beside the headline: a small fraction of trades accounts for more than half of net profit. Factor and benchmark decomposition, together with leave-out diagnostics, continue as an evidence-extension layer for characterizing where historical performance originates.

Return and tail statistics, and their conventions

Reducing a backtest to a few comparable numbers when daily returns are skewed and fat-tailed.

Why a naive method can fail
A Sharpe ratio without its convention cannot be compared across reports. The textbook standard error of a Sharpe ratio assumes independent, normal returns. For a positive Sharpe ratio, fat tails and negative skew make the true error larger, and positive skew makes it smaller.
The method
The Sharpe ratio is the annualized ratio of mean daily return to daily volatility, with no risk-free rate subtracted, so it reads higher than an excess-return Sharpe ratio when rates are positive. Skewness and kurtosis of daily returns enter its sampling error. Drawdown is measured on the daily marked curve. Tail risk is historical value at risk and expected shortfall. On about two years of daily returns, both rest on a small number of tail days.
How it fits the research
The moments change the sampling error of a Sharpe ratio before any correction for multiple trials, and the net direction is computed, not assumed. A Sharpe ratio is read beside the number of trials behind it.

Applied in: Reverse splits faked a short-side result; the repaired result was rejected on its tail.

Concentration of profit

A result can be carried by a handful of trades, and that decides how it should be described.

Why a naive method can fail
Win rate and mean profit per trade look healthy even when a few outcomes carry most of the profit.
The method
The share of net profit from the top fraction of trades, the share of trades needed to reach half of it, the Gini coefficient and Lorenz curve of winning trades’ profit, and a Herfindahl index of absolute profit shares. Leave-out tests recompute the result without the largest winners.
How it fits the research
It produced a limit stated beside the headline: a small fraction of trades accounts for more than half of net profit. The measures are reported for each candidate rule, so a rule that wins by concentrating shows it. The leave-out tests were run on the first reported backtest and are being extended to the selected configuration as part of the continuing robustness program.

Factor attribution by time-series regression

A long-only book in a rising market earns market return by construction. Its profit may be known-factor exposure, not anything specific to the strategy.

Why a naive method can fail
One index misses exposures outside it. Regression residuals on daily returns can be autocorrelated and heteroskedastic, so ordinary standard errors are unreliable for the intercept’s t-statistic.
The method
Ordinary least squares of daily account returns on the market, then on standard factor portfolios from the academic literature and sector indexes, with Newey–West standard errors. It reports the share of profit the factors explain and the uncertainty on the remainder.
How it fits the research
This is after-the-fact attribution of a finished backtest’s returns, and one of the checks I listed for my own results. It forms part of the continuing evidence-extension program used to characterize the sources and robustness of historical performance.

Execution and cost modeling

A flat cost with every order filled at the signal price overstates what a real order earns, so cost is modeled as a function of spread, liquidity, volatility, order size, market impact and fill uncertainty. These methods also put an uncertainty on the weakest input to the model, the impact coefficient, decompose the gap between decision-price profit and executable profit into its causes, and test whether a cleverer order schedule beats a simple one.

Why it matters here. Replacing the flat cost with modeled fills lowered the backtest result, and modeled cost now rises with the simulated starting balance; a price gain seen only under the most favorable fill assumption was not claimed. A formal optimal-control schedule and learned policies were tested against fixed schedules and placebo twins, and none beat the best simple schedule on held-out data, so the simulation keeps the simple rule.

Transaction-cost model and its fill assumptions

A flat cost with every order filled in full at the signal price overstates what a real order earns.

Why a naive method can fail
A flat cost cannot grow with order size or volatility, and cannot leave shares unfilled. A bar’s average price treated as executable hides an error of unknown size and sign.
The method
Trading cost is modeled as a function of spread, liquidity, volatility and order size rather than as a constant per-trade penalty. The research allows market impact and fill uncertainty to vary with trading conditions, and stresses the resulting cost and fill assumptions under alternative conventions, including deliberately adverse cases. The impact calibration remains the model’s least certain input.
How it fits the research
It replaced the flat cost in the backtest, which lowered the result. Modeled cost now rises with the simulated starting balance. A price gain seen only under the most favorable fill assumption was not claimed.

Applied in: Execution and transaction costs.

Estimating the impact coefficient

The impact coefficient sets how fast cost grows with order size. It is the model’s weakest input.

Why a naive method can fail
A textbook value taken as fact hides the uncertainty, and one estimator can report a tight interval around the wrong quantity.
The method
Two regression estimators on public trade-and-volume data, with intervals from resampled sessions. Each is combined with a prior from the academic literature in a one-parameter Bayesian update, its likelihood widened because the data are a proxy.
How it fits the research
The two estimates disagree: the model’s value is conservative against the lower-reading one and about central against the other. Both are estimates from public data; execution data from live operation are now being accumulated to calibrate the coefficient on the strategy’s own orders, and the measurement design is in progress.

Applied in: Execution and transaction costs.

Implementation shortfall decomposition

Profit at the decision price is not profit that can be executed. The gap has several causes, each with a different remedy.

Why a naive method can fail
One slippage number mixes costs with different causes and hides the profit lost on shares that never filled.
The method
Perold’s implementation shortfall, per trade on both legs. Profit at the decision price, less delay, half-spread, impact, fees and the opportunity cost of unfilled shares, equals executable profit, and the identity closes exactly.
How it fits the research
Order-handling rules can be compared on the same trades, component by component. In the execution studies the components add to the total, to the dollar, on every row. That checks the bookkeeping, not the level of any component: each is modeled in the fill simulator.

Applied in: Execution and transaction costs.

Whether a cleverer schedule beats a simple one: optimal control and learned policies

Trading faster costs more impact. Trading slower risks the price moving away or an incomplete order.

Why a naive method can fail
Hand-picked schedules cannot show whether a better one exists. A fitted optimum can lose on held-out data, and a learner can win by rediscovering a fixed setting.
The method
The schedule is posed as a stochastic control problem with temporary market impact and a liquidity constraint: in continuous time its value function satisfies a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation; here it is solved in discrete time as a Bellman recursion, by backward induction, and checked against closed forms, including the Almgren and Chriss linear-impact case. The learned alternative, fitted Q-iteration and contextual bandits, is tested against fixed schedules and placebo twins.
How it fits the research
My condition for a learned policy was that it be shown not to be overfit or a placebo effect. Neither approach beat the best simple schedule on held-out data, so the simulation keeps the simple rule. The execution page has the detail.

Applied in: Execution and transaction costs and Measuring whether ranking was possible before building another ranker.
