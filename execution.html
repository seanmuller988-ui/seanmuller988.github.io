Execution research · October 2026

Execution and transaction costs

A backtest needs a rule for what price an order gets. The easy rule is the bar's closing price, in full, for a small flat fee. This work replaced that rule with a fill model and reports what the model charges.

Performance figures shown on this site are historical backtests. The platform also trades live with real capital, but live performance is not published here. The execution costs on this page are model outputs; live order data is being accumulated as a separate calibration source.

The problem

Real orders do three things the easy rule ignores.

They cross the spread

A buyer pays more than the mid price and a seller receives less.

They move the price

The larger an order is relative to the volume trading at the time, the more it pays.

They may not fill

In thinly traded names only part of an order can be done in the time available.

Method

Standard models. The impact setting is conservative under one estimate and about central under the other.

Cost per side is modeled as half of an assumed bid-ask spread plus a square-root market-impact term:

cost per side  =  s / 2  +  Y · σ · √(Q / V)

s is the spread assumed for the stock's liquidity tier, σ its daily volatility, Q the order size in dollars and V its daily dollar volume. Y is the impact coefficient.

The simulator separates the research decision from the modeled execution process, allows partial fills when liquidity is limited, and decomposes implementation shortfall into delay, spread and impact. Exact scheduling, participation and order-handling rules are intentionally omitted.

The impact setting was estimated from public market data using two approaches and combined with a prior from the academic literature. The estimates disagree, so the difference is treated as model uncertainty rather than resolved away. Live order observations are now being accumulated for a future calibration check. Exact coefficients and execution parameters are not disclosed.

Selected findings

These studies were run on the trade record of the first reported backtest, which is superseded, and have not been repeated on the selected configuration. The cost table further down is the selected configuration.

At small size, participation decides fills, not cost

Captured profit was indistinguishable across the three higher participation rates tested. It was lower at the lowest rate, where more of each order went unfilled, but that gap was not statistically resolved at the base size and was resolved only narrowly at three times the size.

A better exit rule improved completion, not price

Against the first version of the worked-exit rule, a refined version raised the filled share of exit orders under every fill assumption tested. A small price gain appeared only under the most favorable assumption and vanished under a stricter one, so none is claimed.

More complexity did not automatically improve execution

A more sophisticated optimal-control formulation was tested against simpler scheduling rules. On held-out simulation it did not outperform the best simple alternative, so the production research path kept the simpler rule rather than adding complexity without evidence.

Capacity is not one number

In a projection on the first reported trade record, the binding constraint differed across segments of the universe: in some the order outgrew the volume available, in others modeled cost was the limit. Between half-years the levels moved by large factors, so I report no capacity figure. The projection has not been repeated on the selected configuration.

Cost and scale

Modeled execution friction rises as simulated capital increases, but the relationship is not summarized publicly as a single capacity number.

Costs are size-sensitive

Scaling the same research logic to larger simulated orders increases modeled spread, impact and fill constraints. The exact size-to-cost mapping is intentionally omitted.

Capacity varies through time

The binding constraint can shift between available liquidity and modeled cost, and the estimated limit moves materially across periods and parts of the universe.

No single capacity claim

I treat capacity as a research question rather than a fixed headline number. The selected configuration is being re-evaluated under broader execution and live-calibration evidence.

Selected configuration results elsewhere on the site are historical simulations. Configuration selection and evaluation overlap in the primary research window, so those figures are not presented as independent out-of-sample evidence.

What is not fully modeled

  • Live order observations are accumulating, but the public-data impact model remains the principal research calibration until the live sample is large enough for a stable comparison.
  • Financing cost on exposure above the account's equity is not charged in the historical simulations shown here.
  • Permanent market impact is not modeled in the historical replay.
  • Queue position, latency and crowding are not modeled.
  • Some execution assumptions are deliberately conservative; exact thresholds and sizing rules are omitted from the public site.
