Execution and transaction costs

A backtest needs a rule for what price an order gets. The easy rule is the bar's closing price, in full, for a small flat fee. This programme replaced that rule with a fill model and measured what it costs.

All figures on this page are modelled costs inside a historical simulation. None is measured on real orders.

The problem

Real orders do three things the easy rule ignores.

They cross the spread

A buyer pays more than the mid price and a seller receives less.

They move the price

The larger an order is relative to the volume trading at the time, the more it pays.

They may not fill

In thinly traded names only part of an order can be done in the time available.

Method

Standard models, calibrated conservatively.

Cost per side is modelled as half the quoted spread plus a square-root market-impact term:

cost per side  =  s / 2  +  Y · σ · √(Q / V)

s is the spread, σ daily volatility, Q the order size and V daily volume. Y is the impact coefficient.

Orders are evaluated under a liquidity-aware fill model that allows delayed and partial execution rather than assuming every order fills immediately and in full. Implementation shortfall is decomposed into broad cost components for research and sensitivity analysis.

Model parameters are calibrated conservatively from public market data and external priors. Calibration values, execution thresholds and production decision rules are intentionally not published.

Selected findings

Liquidity constraints matter before headline costs do

Fill quality changes materially as available liquidity becomes scarce, so capacity cannot be evaluated from a flat fee or full-fill assumption.

Exits can be worked for little

Working an exit instead of crossing immediately gained about two basis points per order, and price drift after an exit was close to zero.

Optimal-control analysis informed execution research

An optimal-control formulation was used to test the trade-off between urgency, impact and signal decay. The resulting production policy is intentionally omitted.

Capacity differs by liquidity

The least liquid names run out of fills first as the account grows; larger names run out of margin later. Capacity is reported by segment, not as one number.

Cost and capacity

Execution costs and fill constraints increase with deployed capital, and the effect is uneven across liquidity segments.

Scale sensitivity

The execution model is rerun across multiple capital levels rather than assuming that small-account fills scale linearly. Larger simulated books experience higher implementation costs and more binding liquidity constraints.

Capacity is segmented

Less-liquid names become constrained first, while deeper names retain more room to scale. Public materials therefore describe the direction and methodology of the capacity analysis without publishing the exact production thresholds or execution policy.

What is not modelled

  • Impact calibration is based on public market data rather than a long history of the strategy's own institutional-sized orders.
  • Financing cost on exposure above the account's equity is not charged in the historical simulation.
  • Permanent impact and some intraday liquidity dynamics remain outside the current execution model.

Selected materials are presented for educational and professional purposes. Proprietary signal definitions, parameters, portfolio construction rules and execution logic are intentionally omitted.
