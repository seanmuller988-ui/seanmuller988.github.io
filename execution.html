Execution research · October 2026

Execution and transaction costs

A backtest needs a rule for what price an order gets. The easy rule is the price that prompted the order, in full, for a small flat fee. This work replaced it with a cost model built on spread, liquidity, volatility and order size, and asked which conclusions survive.

Every performance figure on this site is a historical backtest. No live or paper-trading results are shown here. Costs on this page are modeled.

The problem

A flat cost with full fills is not conservative. It is wrong in the direction that flatters the result.

Real orders cross part of the bid-ask spread, move the price against themselves by more the larger they are relative to the volume trading at the time, and sometimes cannot be completed, so part of the expected profit is never earned. A flat cost hides all three, says nothing about capacity because it charges the same at every account size, and flatters the least liquid names most. My first reported backtest used it. Replacing it lowered the result materially; I accepted the lower figure because the evidence behind it is more credible.

The modeling framework

Historical execution research here models trading costs as functions of spread, volatility, liquidity and order size rather than assuming a constant per-trade penalty. The framework allows market impact and fill uncertainty to vary with trading conditions, and tests whether the research remains economically meaningful under less favorable assumptions. The impact calibration remains the least certain input and is discussed at a high level in the execution brief.

Around it sit delay, since an order reaches the market after the price that prompted it; partial fills, since an order may fill only in part when volume is thin, with the unfilled remainder carried as an opportunity cost rather than assumed away; and implementation shortfall, which splits the gap between profit at the decision price and executable profit into delay, spread, impact, fees and unfilled shares, so order-handling rules can be compared on the same trades.

The impact coefficient was estimated two ways from public trade-and-volume data and combined with a prior from the academic literature. The estimators disagree, and I carry that as uncertainty: the model’s value is conservative against one and about central against the other.

What the studies found

More than a dozen studies ran on an intraday fill simulator over the first reported trade record, tuned on one half of the sample and read on the other. A finding counted only if it held in at least three of four half-years and on busy as well as ordinary days.

  • At small scale, fill assumptions matter through unfilled shares, not through cost. Stressing the fill assumption over a wide range barely changed captured profit except at the most restrictive setting, where more of each order went unfilled; even that gap resolved only at larger scale.
  • Working an order over a window beat a single immediate order, mostly through fewer unfilled shares. The lead held in every half-year but rests on a small share of trades, is a tie on busy days, and is only marginally significant once dependence within sessions and within groups of related stocks is allowed for.
  • Neither optimal control nor a learned policy beat a simple schedule. Posed as a stochastic control problem with temporary market impact and a liquidity constraint, whose value function satisfies a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation, the schedule turns on how fast the expected gain decays against how convex impact cost is. Its full solution spread most orders, because convex impact makes spreading cheaper once there is time and volume to finish; on held-out data that lost to the best simple schedule, and modest risk aversion drove it back toward that rule. A learned timing policy fared no better: its gain was matched by a copy trained on scrambled inputs.

Capacity

Capacity is not one number: per order, per book and economic capacity answer different questions. In the capacity study the binding constraint differed across segments of the universe, available volume in some and modeled cost in others; between half-years only the first segment to saturate repeated and the levels moved by large factors, so I report no capacity figure.

What I do report is the cost model inside the headline backtest. The half-spread component is flat across scale while impact grows with order size, so modeled cost rises with the simulated account and partial fills become material at larger scale. At a $1M simulated scale it is about 40 basis points per round trip, and the ending multiple after that cost is 3.04×. Selected configuration result. Configuration selection and evaluation overlap in this research window, so it is not independent out-of-sample evidence. Deflated Sharpe ratio: 0.85.

The full write-up is in the execution and transaction-cost brief (PDF).

What is not modeled

  • Permanent impact. My fills do not move the prices that later fills and marks see.
  • Financing. The simulated book can use leverage, and no financing cost is charged.
  • Completion on every leg. Where the fill model assumes an order completes, finer-grained studies showed that at larger scale some did not, so modeled cost there is understated.
  • Volume persistence, queue position, latency and crowding. None is modeled, and the capacity study held profit per trade fixed.

Limits

  • Calibration. Every cost figure here is modeled; the impact coefficient comes from public tape data and a literature prior. Execution data from live operation are now being accumulated to refine and empirically calibrate the impact assumptions used in the historical research; a study of what that measurement requires found that the benchmark price matters more than the number of fills, so the measurement design is in progress. A paper account cannot supply it at any size, because it simulates its fills.
  • Where the studies ran. The detailed studies used the first reported trade record, before the universe was rebuilt as of each date; repeating them on the selected configuration’s trade record is a planned extension (research agenda).
  • Busy days. That half of the repeatability rule rests on few sessions, so a pass or a fail there is weak evidence.
  • Fill-price convention. The fill price is a proxy for an executable price. In an earlier replay on the superseded universe an adverse bound on every fill removed essentially all of the simulated gain: a bound, not a forecast. That stress is part of the planned repeat on the selected configuration.
