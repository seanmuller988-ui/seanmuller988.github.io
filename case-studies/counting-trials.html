Counting the trials behind a deflated Sharpe ratio

A deflated Sharpe ratio had assumed 100 trials. A recount found about 540 runs, the claim was restated as a range, and the next sweep was judged as one family.

Multiple testing Placebo controls Validation

The question

The question was how many trials stood behind a deflated Sharpe ratio I had reported. The first reported historical backtest of my systematic equity strategy, since superseded, was issued with a deflated Sharpe ratio of “1.00” at 100 trials. That statistic is on a probability scale: it is one minus the p-value of the observed Sharpe ratio against the best that luck alone would be expected to produce from the variants tried. It is not the probability that the strategy has skill, and too low a count overstates the evidence. The 100 was not a count: it came from a ladder of preset values in the report generator.

Initial approach

Printing a deflated figure looked like the careful step, and the count went unexamined. In June 2026, for an earlier line of work, I had decided to deflate across the full ledger of searched configurations. This line had no such ledger.

What broke

On 2 October 2026 I listed the checks an outside reviewer would run against my reported results. One item was true trial accounting, where I expected far more than 100. The recount found about 540 runs in 62 hypothesis families, and that “1.00” was a probability of 0.997, not a reduced Sharpe ratio.

Investigation

The 171 runs whose daily return series were still on file correlate 0.88 on average, so among those runs the effective number of independent trials is about 1 to 30, depending on the estimator; the raw count is 540. Neither extreme is the right count. For each count the study computed a Bonferroni-adjusted p-value (p times the number of trials) and the deflated Sharpe probability, which said little alone: the runs are near-copies, so it barely moves with the count.

What changed

The count is now stated as a range, and the statistic is labeled a probability. For the October research sweep I required a named placebo in every pre-registration (a plan written before results), and every configuration was counted: more than 10,000. A literature scan I had asked for, to find methods I might not know, recommended a correlation-aware family-wise test (Reality Check and SPA with a Romano–Wolf step-down): one verdict on whether the best of many correlated candidates could be luck. All 69 candidates with a per-session return series entered it, beside 44 placebo twins: selection-type candidates with their information shuffled out.

Validation

The twins gave no rejections (family-wise p 0.91). That is what a null family should give, though a single reading shows only that the test did not reject where it should not. A rule written into the test’s plan classed a verdict that flips across bootstrap block lengths as unstable, not significant. One limit: the test’s plan, like the written entry of one other of the sweep’s 18 studies, entered version control together with its results, so version control cannot show that it predates them.

The framework in use. I had asked for a revision to how the liquidity screen is measured. It was pre-registered with a modest hypothesis: that the revision would cost nothing in trades or profit. Full historical backtests then seemed to show more: a materially higher ending multiple with modeled costs than the original configuration’s 1.87× (Historical backtest · original configuration, same realism). A research-log entry called it the rule to bring forward.

A panel for that candidate then could not separate the two (adjusted p about 0.35), and the entry was corrected in writing as premature. Three placebo versions (mechanics kept, information randomized, one seed each) and a like-for-like control were run, under a panel written after the direction of its main comparison was known.

Result

The first reported claim got weaker. The Sharpe ratio of 2.32 (Archived · historical backtest, first reported, superseded) survives a 5% Bonferroni correction for up to 55 independent trials: not at the 100 assumed (adjusted p about 0.09), nor at 540; its deflated Sharpe probability is 0.997 at 100 trials and 0.995 at 540 (0.97 on the wider spread of all runs).

The sweep claimed no new lever. One of 69 candidates survived on the whole sample. It was an effect already known; it held at the primary block length and the next (adjusted p 0.005 and 0.04), not at the two longest (0.14 and 0.21), and not in the later half of the sample alone. Planted effects of two realistic per-trade sizes were found 20% and 31% of the time at the studies’ own thresholds (29% and 40% under the family-wise rule): the sample, not the threshold, limited discovery.

The revised screen earns no credit. Its modest hypothesis held; the stronger claim was withdrawn. Most placebo runs ended level with it or above, before and after costs, as did the like-for-like control before costs, and the re-scored panel rejected nothing. The log credits what improvement there was to the level of the minimum-liquidity requirement, not to the revised measurement, and that level was itself chosen after testing five settings on the same window.

The selected configuration, whose figures and qualifier are on the platform page, still uses the revised measurement, so part of its gap from the original configuration’s 1.87× is not claimed as skill. Its deflated Sharpe ratio of 0.85 is a probability, measured against the 240 full-window backtests on file, fewer than the 540 runs the recount found.

What I learned

A deflated statistic is only as good as the count behind it, and I had reported one without checking the count. The ledger rule I had set in June did not carry over to this line of work by itself. A gap between two single simulations of a compounding book is not evidence unless it exceeds what small, arbitrary changes do to a two-year multiple.

What changed in the research process afterward

The rule adopted: count every run, state the count as a range, and judge related tests once as a family, with null twins inside and the test’s power stated. That rule has been exercised once, on the October sweep. The sweep’s closing report wrote it into the rules for the next sweep, which is being designed.

The revised-screen episode added another, which the record so far shows applied only in that correction: a candidate is not named until it has beaten a version of itself with the information removed.
