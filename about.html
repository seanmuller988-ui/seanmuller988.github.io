What I work on

The questions I find most interesting are about evidence: whether a result is real, what it would take to show that it is not, and how much a given sample can resolve. The quantitative methods page describes the tools I use for that, the case studies are where it shows, and the research agenda lists the questions I want to answer next.

I am as interested in the trading side as in the statistics: what an idea is worth once an order has to be worked, why a fill differs from the price that prompted it, and what the book is exposed to while positions are open and capital sits idle.

Most of my time goes into one systematic equity strategy and the research platform around it, described on the platform page.

Why I built the research platform

Much of the platform grew out of questions I wanted to answer rather than features I planned to build. I wanted to see the whole research lifecycle at first hand: data, hypothesis, test, cost, portfolio, order, and the gap between what a simulation says and what a trading engine does.

How I approach learning

The research standards on the methods page are the longer record: each one dates from an event where I was wrong, or a test was. Two shorter examples follow.

In June 2026 I declined a proposed power analysis: small samples, I reasoned, were a symptom of defects. In July, in a separate line of work, a backtest result that had tested high came back low on fresh data, and I assumed an error in the data or the processing. Three checks found none. The gap was inside two standard errors, and the high figure had been the best of about 45 configurations. The rule recorded in August: a null from an underpowered test is recorded as underpowered, not as failed, and a report states the smallest effect its sample could detect.

Working on execution, I came across the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation while searching for my own problem, and in October 2026 I had it tested as a formulation of the order schedule. The schedule it produced lost to the best simple schedule on held-out data. The same week I set a rule for the machine-learning work: every method tested comes with a plain statement of its goal, its inputs and how it can fail, so that none is a black box to me.

How the work is done

The research questions, methodology, system design, and interpretation are independently directed; implementation uses extensive AI-assisted development. Orders reach the broker through a separate order service that the trading engine integrates with.

The work is only as reliable as its controls, and a control is useful only if it can fail: placebos, parity proofs, plans written before results, and independent re-checks of anything that looks too good. Those checks do not replace outside review, which the work has not yet had.

What I am working to get better at

These are gaps the work itself has exposed, and the ones I most want to close beside more experienced researchers.

  • Inference when the effective sample is small: overlapping trades, dependent days and many trials on one two-year window.
  • Measuring execution cost on the strategy’s own orders, as execution data from live operation accumulate.
  • The mathematics behind the machine-learning and stochastic-control methods I test: enough depth to derive them, not only to judge their results.
  • Portfolio-level attribution: separating market and factor exposure from what is specific to the strategy, which was run on the first reported trade record and is being extended to the selected configuration as part of the continuing robustness program.
